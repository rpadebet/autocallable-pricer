Autocallable Structured Products

🏦What Is an Autocallable?

Think of it like a savings CD with a surprise early exit option. The bank says: "Give us $1,000. We'll pay you 8% a year. But if the stock market is above where it started at any check-in date, we'll give you your $1,000 back early — plus coupon for the time you held it. If markets crash hard (say, below 75%), you share in the loss at the end."
What it is
A structured note that automatically redeems early (autocalls) if the underlying index hits a trigger level on a scheduled observation date. Investors get an above-market coupon in exchange for taking on downside risk.
Key mechanics
Call barrier: If index ≥ this % of starting level → product redeems early + coupon

Coupon barrier: If index ≥ this level → coupon is paid (even if not called)

Knock-in barrier: If index falls below this → investor bears losses at maturity

Scenarios See the payoff diagram — drag the sliders to see how price changes with spot, vol, and barrier levels.

The Volatility Surface

📈Implied Volatility & the Vol Smile

Imagine options are insurance policies for a stock. A policy protecting against a small drop costs roughly the same as a policy protecting against a large drop — right? No. In reality, options protecting against crashes are disproportionately expensive. This difference, when you "back out" a volatility number that explains each price, creates a curve called the "volatility smile."
What it is
The Black-Scholes model assumes stock volatility is constant. But real market option prices imply different volatility levels at different strikes and maturities. Plotting these implied vols creates a 3D surface — the vol surface.
Why it matters for autocallables
Autocallables have barrier conditions at specific strikes and maturities. Using the wrong vol (e.g., a flat 20% when the market implies 28% at the relevant strike) misprices the product. The vol surface lets the pricer use the "right" volatility for each scenario.
Try it
Vol Surface Tab "3D Implied Vol." Rotate the surface. Notice that far out-of-the-money puts (left of ATM) have higher implied vol than at-the-money options — this is the equity "skew."

🌀Heston Model — "Volatility Has Its Own Mood Swings"

In real markets, volatility itself changes — it spikes when markets panic and calms down in quiet periods. The Heston model says: "Let's treat volatility like another random process with its own mean, its own speed of reverting to normal, and its own randomness."
What it models
Volatility follows a "rubber band" process — it can stretch away from its average level but is always pulled back. The key parameters are: κ (how strongly it snaps back), θ (the long-run average vol level), γ (how wild vol-of-vol is), and ρ (correlation between stock moves and vol moves).
ρ (rho) is important
For equities, when markets fall sharply, vol tends to spike. This negative correlation (ρ ≈ −0.70) creates the skew seen in the vol surface. Heston captures this naturally — flat-vol models cannot.
Try it
Vol Surface Tab "Heston Overlay." Click "Calibrate Heston" — the model fits its 5 parameters to market prices. The dashed model surface should lie close to the solid market surface. Red patches = fit error.

💥Merton Jump Diffusion — "Markets Occasionally Have Car Crashes"

Standard models assume stock prices move like a person taking tiny random steps all day. The Merton model adds the possibility that occasionally a bus runs the person over — a sudden large drop (or rise) that no amount of small steps could explain. These are "jumps."
What it adds
On top of normal day-to-day random moves, adds a Poisson process — random "events" (crashes, gap moves) that arrive at rate λ (e.g., 0.1 = roughly one crash per 10 years), each with a jump size drawn from a normal distribution.
Why it matters
Jumps create a steeper vol skew for short maturities. For autocallables with barrier conditions, a sudden gap down through the knock-in barrier is exactly the kind of risk jumps model. Ignoring jumps underprices this tail risk.
Try it
Vol Surface Tab "Model Comparison." Click "Calibrate All Models." Compare Merton's RMSE vs Heston — if Merton fits better at short maturities, jump risk dominates there.

🔀Bates Model — "Heston + Jumps Combined"

If Heston is "vol has moods" and Merton is "crashes happen," Bates says both are true simultaneously. Markets have mean-reverting vol AND occasional crash events. It's more realistic but also has 8 parameters to calibrate — with great power comes great complexity.
What it is
Combines Heston's stochastic variance process with Merton's Poisson jump component. The result fits real option surfaces more accurately, especially the short-term smile that Heston alone can struggle with.
Trade-off
More parameters → better fit, but harder to calibrate and prone to overfitting sparse data. The app warms up Bates calibration from the Heston solution to speed convergence. Check the Feller condition — if violated, vol can go negative (bad).
Try it
Vol Surface Model Comparison tab → Calibrate All. The smile chart shows all three models vs. market at 1y and 2y maturities. Bates usually wins on RMSE but is the most expensive to compute.

🗺️Dupire Local Volatility — "A Different Volatility at Every Point"

Instead of one overall volatility personality (flat), or a random personality (Heston), Dupire asks: "What exact volatility at each specific (price, time) point would make all option prices match perfectly?" It's like drawing a custom weather map for every location and time rather than assuming the same weather everywhere.
What it does
Mathematically "inverts" the observed option prices to extract a volatility that is a function of both the stock price level (S) and time (t). Written as σ(S,t). When used in Monte Carlo, each simulated stock path uses the volatility appropriate for where the stock is right now.
How it's computed
Uses Dupire's formula on a smooth interpolation of the implied vol surface. The app builds a 40×25 grid of σ(S,t) values at trade, then uses fast interpolation during simulation so the pricer doesn't slow down.
Try it
Vol Surface Tab "Dupire Local Vol." Compare it to the implied vol surface on the left — local vol looks "flatter" in the strike dimension because it averages over future paths. Steep areas = high vol of vol.

Pricing Methods

⬅️Finite Difference Method (FDM) — "Solving Backwards From Maturity"

Imagine you know what the prize is at the end of a maze (the payoff at maturity). FDM works backwards — from the known ending, it figures out what the value must be at each earlier point. It's like filling in a spreadsheet from right to left, row by row.
How it works
Sets up a grid: stock price (vertical) × time (horizontal). Starting from maturity (where we know the payoff), it uses a mathematical update rule at each time step, working backwards to the starting date. The value at today's stock price is the product's fair value.
Two schemes
Explicit: Simple update, but requires small time steps to stay stable (like a bicycle that tips over if you go too fast around corners).

Crank-Nicolson: Blends the current and next time step. More accurate and stable for the same grid size — costs slightly more per step but worth it.
Try it
FDM Viz Watch the "heatmap" — brighter colors are higher value. See how value evolves backward in time, with the autocall barrier appearing as a sharp edge (values above the barrier get "called away"). Use the time-slice animation to watch it sweep.

🎲Standard Monte Carlo — "Rolling Dice 10,000 Times"

To find the average outcome of a card game, you could calculate it exactly (hard), or just play the game 10,000 times and take the average (easy). Monte Carlo is the second approach — simulate thousands of possible stock price paths and average the payoffs.
How it works
Generates thousands of random stock price "futures" using the vol model selected (flat, local, Heston, or Bates). For each path, checks if the autocall triggered on any observation date and computes the discounted payoff. Averages all payoffs to get the price.
The problem: noise
With 1,000 paths the estimate can be off by several dollars. With 100,000 paths it's much more accurate but takes 10× longer. The confidence interval (shown as a band around the price line) tells you how reliable the estimate is.
Try it
Pricer Convergence chart: watch how the price estimate and confidence band narrow as N (paths) increases. With Antithetic on vs. off — notice the band narrows faster with antithetic.

🎯One-Step Survival Monte Carlo — "Loaded Dice for Better Accuracy"

In standard MC, paths that hit the autocall barrier "die" early and are discarded. This wastes a lot of computation. Survival MC asks: "Instead of letting paths randomly hit the barrier, what if we mathematically account for the probability of hitting it at each step, and then only simulate paths that survive?" You get the same answer with far fewer paths.
The key idea
At each observation date, instead of asking "did we hit the barrier?" and stopping, it computes the probability of hitting the barrier and factors that into the payoff mathematically. All paths run to maturity — no early stops. The result: smoother estimates with smaller error bands.
Why it's better
The variance (noise) of the estimate drops dramatically. Especially important for products near the barrier where many standard MC paths hit and get terminated, making the estimate unreliable. Survival MC gets the same accuracy with 5–10× fewer paths.
Try it
Pricer Convergence chart — Standard MC vs Survival MC side by side. At the same N, Survival MC's confidence band (lighter colored region) is visibly narrower. This IS the variance reduction claim from the paper.

🔀Antithetic Variates — "Every Path Gets a Mirror Twin"

If you flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads, you know you've been "lucky" and the estimate is biased upward. Antithetic variates say: for every random sequence used to simulate a path, also simulate the exact opposite sequence. Average the two. The lucky and unlucky paths cancel each other out, giving a better estimate for free.
How it works
For every random number z used to simulate a path, also run a path using −z. The two paths are "mirrors" of each other. Their payoffs are averaged before being combined with other pairs. Roughly halves the standard error for the same number of paths.
Limitation
Only works for the flat-vol (Black-Scholes) model. With Heston or Bates, the variance SDE is non-linear — pairing a path with its mirror no longer guarantees they cancel properly. The app auto-disables antithetic for non-flat models.
Try it
Sidebar ⑤ Toggle "Antithetic Variates" on/off. With Flat vol, run the Pricer and compare Standard MC standard error (shown in the price tab) — it should drop by ~√2 with antithetic on.

🔢Call Probability Table — "When Is This Thing Likely to Autocall?"

Not all observation dates are equally likely to trigger the autocall. For a product with quarterly observations, the first quarter might have a 35% chance of calling (if markets are near the barrier), but by month 12, only 2% of paths are still alive and uncalled. The call probability table maps this out.
What it shows
For each observation date: probability that the product first autocalls on that specific date (not on any earlier date). Sum of all probabilities = total autocall probability. Remainder = probability of running to maturity.
How it's computed
Uses the PDE/analytical approach from Paper 1 (Deng et al.) — exploits the joint lognormal distribution of returns between observation dates to compute exact probabilities without running a full Monte Carlo.
Try it
Pricer FDM tab → "Call Probability at Each Observation Date" table. Then go to Scenarios → same table. Try moving the spot slider toward the call barrier — watch the early call probabilities jump.

Greeks & Stable Differentiation

🌡️What Are Greeks?

If your house price goes up $1,000 for every 1% rise in local incomes, your house has a "Delta" of $1,000 per 1% income change. Greeks are exactly this — sensitivities. How much does the option value change if I move one input a tiny bit?
Delta (Δ)
How much the product value changes if the stock moves $1. A Delta of 0.4 means: if SPX goes from 5,000 to 5,001, the product gains about $0.40. Traders use Delta to hedge — they hold a position in the stock equal in size to minus the Delta, so moves cancel out.
Vega (ν)
How much the product value changes if implied volatility moves 1 percentage point. Autocallables are typically short vega — they lose value when vol rises. Banks selling these products need to hedge their vega exposure in the options market.
Try it
Greeks Look at the "Delta smile" chart — Delta vs. spot level. It's not a straight line, which means the hedge ratio changes as the stock moves. This non-linearity is called "Gamma."

⚡The Barrier Problem — Why Standard MC Greeks Are Unreliable

Imagine you're trying to measure the height of a cliff edge by poking around with a stick. Right at the cliff edge, one inch forward versus one inch backward gives wildly different results (ground vs. air). Option barriers are the same — the payoff function has a "cliff edge" at the barrier, making numerical derivatives (Greeks) very noisy near it.
The problem
Standard Monte Carlo computes Delta by: price at S₀+ε minus price at S₀−ε, divided by 2ε. But near the autocall barrier, different paths cross the barrier depending on which bump direction you use, causing the estimate to jump wildly between large positive and negative values.
The Survival MC solution
Because Survival MC analytically handles barrier crossing (no path ever "hits" the barrier — instead it computes the probability), the payoff function becomes smooth with respect to the starting spot. Bumping S₀ by ε gives reliable, smooth derivative estimates.
Try it
Greeks "Delta Stability" chart: left panel = Standard MC (spiky, unreliable near barrier), right panel = Survival MC (smooth, monotone). This IS the key selling point of the Alm et al. paper.
